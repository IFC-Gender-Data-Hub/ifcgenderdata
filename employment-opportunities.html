Beyond Employment Numbers: Understanding Women's Experiences at Work

Women’s participation in paid work has increased, but employment levels tell only part of the story. Where women work, the roles they hold, and how their employment changes over time also shape their experiences, including the occupations and sectors in which they work and the stability of their employment. Research has documented persistent differences in the types of work available to women and men and in the opportunities associated with different jobs (World Bank 2012; Blau and Kahn 2017; ILO 2024). International labour statistics provide an important picture of these patterns across countries and industries, while looking within firms offers another perspective on workforce composition, changes in employment over time, and what companies report doing to support women at work.

Women’s Workforce Representation

Women’s share of employment has increased over the period, although it remained below parity. Comparing women’s share of all employees with their share of entry-level employees shows whether women are represented similarly at the starting level of firms. The pattern varies by region. By 2025, women’s share of entry-level employees was below their share of the overall workforce in the global series and in East Asia and Pacific, Europe and Central Asia, Latin America and the Caribbean, and North America. In the Middle East, North Africa, Afghanistan and Pakistan, South Asia, and Sub-Saharan Africa, the entry-level share was higher. These regional and country-level differences are consistent with broader evidence of persistent gender disparities in workforce representation, underscoring that women’s overall employment share does not fully capture where they are positioned within the workforce (Liepmann and Hegewisch 2025).

Where Women Work and the Jobs They Hold

Geographic differences are only one dimension of women’s representation. The industries in which women work also vary considerably. Industries are grouped according to the letter-level sections of the International Standard Industrial Classification of All Economic Activities (ISIC), which provides a common framework for comparing economic activities across sectors. Women account for larger shares of employment in sectors such as health and social work, education, and some consumer services, while their representation is lower in construction, mining and quarrying, and other industrial sectors.

Where women work also depends on the occupations they hold. Occupations are grouped according to the nine major groups of the International Standard Classification of Occupations (ISCO), which provides a common framework for comparing types of work. Across all firm-size groups, professional occupations account for the largest share of profiled women’s employment, followed by managerial and technician and associate professional occupations. The occupational mix, however, differs by firm size, with professional roles accounting for a larger share of women’s profiled employment in large firms and managerial roles accounting for a larger share in small firms.

Women’s Employment Dynamics

The occupational profiles show the types of roles women hold, while hiring rates offer another perspective by showing how women and men enter firms. Across most regions and for much of the period, women’s hiring rates are higher than men’s when measured relative to their respective employment bases. This pattern shows that new hires represent a larger share of women’s existing employment base than of men’s, although the difference varies across regions and over time. Research suggests that gender differences in recruitment are closely related to occupational composition, with hiring outcomes varying across male- and female-dominated occupations (Adamovic and Leibbrandt 2023; Lippens et al. 2023).

Beyond who enters firms, the length of time employees remain with their employers offers another perspective on employment patterns. Retention measures the share of women who remain with the same employer after twelve months. Comparing hiring and retention rates across sectors highlights differences in both workforce entry and employment continuity. For example, Arts, Entertainment and Recreation and Accommodation and Food Services have among the highest hiring rates in the chart, but also among the lowest retention rates. By contrast, Finance and Insurance records a relatively lower hiring rate alongside one of the highest retention rates. This distinction is also relevant in the literature on job stability and employee turnover. Hom et al. (2008), drawing on data from 20 corporations, document differences in employee turnover by gender and show that turnover patterns vary with employees’ length of service. Similarly, Hollister and Smith (2014) find that trends in job tenure differ between women and men and are shaped by changes in employment patterns over time.

What Firms Report on Gender Equality

The differences in women’s representation across sectors and occupations raise a further question: what do companies report doing to support women at work? The literature highlights the role of firm-level policies and practices in promoting equal opportunities across recruitment, training, retention, and career progression (IFC 2013; ILO 2025). Against this background, a large majority of listed companies report policies providing equal access to training and career development and equal-opportunity hiring. By contrast, employee resource groups for women are reported by a much smaller share of companies.

Expanding women’s economic opportunities requires more than increasing their participation in employment. It also means creating workplaces where women have opportunities to develop their skills, advance professionally, and sustain meaningful employment over time. The ways organizations recruit, develop, and retain employees, together with the policies they put in place to support gender inclusion, shape the broader landscape of opportunities available to women. Moving from commitments to meaningful workplace inclusion requires attention not only to who gets hired, but also to the conditions, opportunities, and support that enable women to thrive throughout their working lives.
